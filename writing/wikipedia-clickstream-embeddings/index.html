Turning Wikipedia clickstream into a taste space

Olima looks like a game about picking celebrities. Under the hood, it's a recommender system navigating a 64-dimensional embedding space built from how millions of people actually browse.

Behavior beats taxonomy

If you want to know which famous people are "similar," you have two options. Build it from a knowledge graph — occupations, nationalities, awards — or build it from behavior: the Wikipedia clickstream dataset, which records how readers actually navigate from one article to another.

I chose behavior, and the difference shows immediately. A knowledge graph thinks Messi's nearest neighbors are "other Argentine forwards." The clickstream knows what humans know: Messi's #1 neighbor is Cristiano Ronaldo, at 0.9967 similarity in our space. Nobody curated that. Millions of people clicking from one page to the other encoded it for us.

From the co-occurrence structure of those navigation paths, I compute spectral embeddings — 64 dimensions per entity. Every person becomes a point; closeness means people behave as if these two belong together.

Entities need stable names

One unglamorous decision that paid for itself: entities are keyed by Wikidata QIDs, not internal IDs. Barack Obama is Q76 whether the embeddings are v3 or v7. Analytics survive model upgrades; user profiles can be re-projected into new spaces; nothing breaks when the geometry improves.

The game is a candidate selector

Each round shows five rows of people and asks who you'd sit next to. Each row is a geometric move relative to your current profile vector:

RowGeometric definitionPurpose
SimilarClosest to current profileContinue the current direction
DifferentOpposite direction from SimilarExplore the opposite region
Diverse A & BMaximize tetrahedron volume with the first twoSpread — avoid collapsing into one cluster
HubGeometric center, popularity-weightedAlways one recognizable rescue anchor

Whoever you tap in a row, you've chosen that row's direction — the celebrity is just the friendly face on a vector. Your profile then evolves by exponential moving average:

profile = 0.7 * profile + 0.3 * selection

History outweighs any single pick (70/30), so the profile converges smoothly instead of whiplashing — the same intuition as learning-rate tuning, applied to identity.

Deterministic where possible, generative where valuable

The selection engine is pure pre-computed geometry — no LLM in the loop. A full session costs about $0.002, which is the entire reason an unlimited free tier is viable. The LLMs enter only where they create magic: a mascot agent (GPT-5) that reads your trajectory through the space and narrates what your taste says about you, and an asset pipeline (Gemini) for visuals.

This split — deterministic engine, generative narrator — is, I think, the most underrated pattern in consumer AI right now. The geometry does the understanding. The LLM does the telling. Each is doing the only job it's actually best at, and the unit economics work because of it.

Takeaways

  • Behavioral co-occurrence is a free, massive signal. Clickstreams encode human similarity judgments at a scale no curation can match.
  • Stable entity IDs are an architecture decision, not a detail. QIDs decoupled the product from any particular embedding version.
  • Don't pay an LLM to do linear algebra. If the operation is geometric, pre-compute it; spend tokens on language.
